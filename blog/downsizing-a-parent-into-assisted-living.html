Downsizing a parent into assisted living

By the Hero’s team. Updated August 2026. A 7 minute read.

A studio or one-bedroom in assisted living holds a fraction of what a three-bedroom house held. Most families end up moving about a room's worth of belongings and deciding what happens to everything else. The order that works is this: take the move-in list first, then the family and paperwork, then donations, then the haul. Doing it in that order is what keeps anything from getting thrown out by mistake.

Nobody does this on a good week. It usually starts with a fall, a diagnosis, or a phone call from a neighbor, and then a family has three weeks to empty a house that took forty years to fill.

Start with what actually fits in the new room

Before anyone sorts a single closet, get the floor plan and the community's own move-in rules from the facility. Most of them publish a list. Bed size, whether you may bring a recliner, whether a microwave or a space heater is allowed, what furniture the room already includes.

That list is short and it is the most useful piece of paper in this whole process, because it converts a vague question about a house into a specific question about one room. The National Institute on Aging has a plain overview of the different care levels and what each one actually provides at long-term care, which is worth reading if the family is still deciding between options.

If you have not picked a community yet, your regional Area Agency on Aging is the free, non-commercial place to start. The federal directory of them is at Area Agencies on Aging. They do not sell anything.

The sort, in the order that avoids regret

Four passes through the house, each one narrower than the last.

  • Pass one, the move-in list. Only the items going to the new room. Tag them, move them to one room, and stop touching them. This is the smallest pile and the only one with a deadline.
  • Pass two, paper and small valuables. Deeds, titles, insurance policies, military records, tax files, jewelry, coin books, photographs, anything in a safe or a lockbox. Family handles this pass. No hauler, no estate company, nobody outside the family.
  • Pass three, family. Siblings and grandchildren take what they want, in person, with a deadline attached. A shared list works better than a group text.
  • Pass four, everything else. Donation, recycling, and disposal. This is the pass we handle.

Pass two is the one people rush and later regret. Paperwork hides in the same three places in almost every house: a filing drawer, a dresser drawer under folded clothes, and inside books. Go through them slowly.

Nothing leaves until the family says it can. We do not start a room until someone walks it with us first and points. A crew that starts loading before that conversation is a crew that will carry something out of the house that should have stayed in it.

What we take, and what we leave alone

We take furniture that is not going with them, mattresses, appliances, televisions and electronics, patio furniture, garage contents, workshop leftovers, boxes of household goods, and whole rooms once the family has cleared them. Furniture is usually the bulk of it, and the details of that work are on furniture removal. The whole-house version is estate cleanouts.

We do not take household hazardous waste. Paint, stain, thinner, motor oil, gasoline, propane tanks, pool chemicals, pesticides, and old solvents come out of a garage in almost every one of these jobs. Those go to your city's collection program, which is free for residents. Plano runs household chemical collection, and every city around here has an equivalent that we will point you to.

We also leave firearms, ammunition, prescription medication, and anything in a locked container to the family. Those have their own rules and they are not a hauling job.

The donation question, answered honestly

A lot of what comes out of a parent's house is genuinely good. Solid wood furniture, working appliances, dishes, linens, tools.

If the family has time and a truck, the free route is real. A Habitat for Humanity ReStore takes furniture, appliances, building materials, and household goods, and many locations will schedule a pickup for larger items. Each store publishes its own accepted list, so check before you load anything.

We would rather tell you that and lose part of the job than take money you did not have to spend. When there is no time, and in these situations there usually is not, the usable items ride with us and go to local donation partners rather than into a disposal load. Where your junk goes after we haul it walks through how that sorting works after a load leaves the driveway. If the move is in the other direction, a parent coming home with equipment rather than leaving, the job is clearing a room for a hospital bed or a wheelchair instead.

One caution worth saying plainly. We are haulers, not appraisers. If there is a possibility something in the house has real value, get someone qualified to look before anything moves. We will not pretend to know what a piece is worth.

What the city will take at the curb

Some of this can go through the normal residential system if you have time. Most cities in the area run a bulky waste program for large household items on a schedule, with limits on volume and a list of what they will not accept. Plano publishes its rules at bulky waste collection.

The catch is the calendar. Bulky pickup runs on the city's schedule, not on yours, and a house that has to be empty by the end of the month rarely lines up with it. In an HOA neighborhood, a pile that sits at a curb for two weeks waiting on a collection day usually generates a letter.

How a downsizing job runs on our end

Someone from the family walks the house with us and points at what goes. We give a firm number before anything is lifted, and that number does not move once we start. Disposal is included in it, so the quote is the bill.

Our own crews do the carrying, not gig labor hired off an app that morning. That matters more here than on any other job we do, because these are occupied homes with family paperwork on the counter and somebody's mother sitting in the next room.

We run pickups seven days a week across Plano, Allen, Frisco, McKinney, Richardson, Little Elm, Prosper, The Colony, Wylie, and Fairview. If the move-in date is close, tell us that when you write, because scheduling around a facility's date is normal for us. Tell us what you have and we will quote it before we load anything.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a full house take?

The hauling is usually one visit, sometimes two on a larger home. The family sorting almost always takes longer than the hauling, which is why we suggest starting the paperwork pass weeks before you book anyone.

Can you come in stages?

Yes. Garage first, then the rooms after the family has been through them, is a common way to run it. Each visit is quoted on its own.

What if we find something we want to keep after the crew starts?

Say so and it comes off the load. Nothing is final until it is on the trailer, and we would rather stop and ask than guess.

Do you handle the old medication and the paint in the garage?

No. Medication goes to a pharmacy take-back and hazardous chemicals go to your city's household collection program. We will tell you which one covers your address.

Is there a checklist for the whole process?

Yes. Our estate cleanout checklist covers the same ground in a room-by-room order and works for a downsizing move as well as an estate.
